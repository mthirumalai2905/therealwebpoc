There are two different kinds of web here. They can sit on the same Internet. They are not the same job.

The **World Wide Web** is what most people use today. It is built for documents. You click a link. A server sends a page back. You ask, it answers. That is good for a news article. It is a poor fit for “tell me the second the coffee maker finishes.”

The **Real Time Web** is built for live things: sensors, machines, applications, AI agents, and people using those resources. Instead of ask and wait, the relationship stays open. Authorized updates can arrive as they happen. The link works both ways.

RTW is not meant to replace the WWW. A site can still publish documents. RTW can carry the live channel beside that site.

## A lamp on the network

Take a smart lamp. It has a light and a switch. Treat it as an endpoint: the actual network resource. The walkthrough below builds the vocabulary as that lamp participates. It is a teaching story, not a product click path.

## 1. Identity: the ghost

When the lamp can participate, it is paired with a **ghost**: a shared-memory stand-in for that one resource. What the lamp reports, the ghost can carry. What the ghost is authorized to receive, the lamp can act on.

The ghost is tied to the **resource**, not to a human avatar. You are not the ghost. You may own the ghostId.

The pairing is **bijective**. One lamp, one ghost. One ghost, that lamp. Never one ghost shared by two endpoints.

The ghost provides identity, behavior, and context when an active connection is present. That is different from a stored twin that sits in a database whether the lamp is on or off. RTW does not natively store data in the network.

## 2. A home: the Morphic service

The lamp still needs an application-layer home. That home is a **Morphic service**: a service on MAD that allocates ghosts.

When the service is created, the service owner is the primary owner of those ghosts. When you register and confirm a transfer, you own the **data layer**. The service owner keeps the **application layer**. They may keep a local copy of the data.

The service describes its own data model. In the source that mini description is a **micropage**. RTW is protocol independent. The service owner chooses the encoding. The sketch below is only a local model, not a required schema language.

## 3. Someone else wants the live reading: morphing

Suppose a scheduler service wants to use the lamp at dusk. That service does not need a custom API into the lamp. The owner **morphs** the lamp ghost against the secondary service. Two things happen at once:

1. The scheduler gets **read access**. The owner can withdraw it.
2. The data is **reshaped** into the scheduler’s local model. Each service only sees its own context. How it was morphed is unknown outside that service.

A temperature example from the same idea: several sensor services can feed one user’s channel. One service may keep the reading as it arrives. Another may morph it to fit a different local model. The sensor does not have to change.

## 4. Who is allowed to see it: ownership

Here is the rule that makes this unlike a normal public API.

Data is consumed at an endpoint when the ghosts at the ends are owned by the **same user**, or the receiver has approved **read access**. Same user usually means one person and two of their endpoints, such as their lamp and their display. It does not mean two different people are the same person.

Two different people do not open each other’s data because they share an implementation. A morphs (read) or transfers (ownership).

Ownership sits on the **ghostId**. Whoever owns the ghostId owns the data at that endpoint. A ghost can be transferred. The user has to confirm. After confirm, the new holder has the data layer. That is a change of ownership, not a casual share.

A change in one layer does not have to redefine the whole relationship. The company that hosts a box does not have to own the reading. The person who wrote the scheduler does not have to own the lamp. You do not have to run the network in order to decide who may see the data.

Anyone can develop an implementation of MAD or RTW. This site does not name a vendor.

## Why bother

Today, two live things usually meet through a custom API. Each pairing brings its own interface, often its own login, and a copied dataset. Multiply that by many device types and many applications, and the integrations pile up.

RTW’s pitch is a shared architecture for the link itself. Keep data with the source. Morph it into local models for whoever is authorized. Let ownership stay split across layers.

## Live value, not a stored dump

The network does not natively store data. A service may attach a database to **local** ghosts after it has received a morph. That local copy is the service’s context. It is not a required central store in the middle of the path.

Machines that act on a live reading need the value now. A page you retrieve later is a different job. WWW is strong at the second. RTW is aimed at the first.

## Bijective, in one sentence

Bijective means a clean one-to-one pairing, reversible, with nothing left over. Every participating endpoint maps to exactly one ghost, and that ghost maps back to exactly that endpoint. If a ghost could stand for two lamps at once, ownership of the data would stop being answerable.
